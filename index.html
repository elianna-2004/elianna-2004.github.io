Brief Write-Up of Visualization:


Team Members: Elianna, Eric

White Hat Visualization Explanation:

Our White Hat Visualization hones the essence of truth and honesty. It depicts the whole data in a truthful lighting, with the absence of any cherry picking. Let us break it down:
  • dataset is all the OECD aggregate observations from 2015–2024.
  • The x-axis depicts the years from 2015 to 2024.
  • The y-axis shows the GHG emissions per person (thousand kg CO₂e) from 9 kg to 13 kg because this range isolates the complete data. This allows us to see the fluctuation changes in greater detail, providing more transparency to the audience.
  • When it comes to design choice, we opted out for a line graph to show historical changes throughout the years to help the audience visually see the changes and show how emissions have decreased over time. We believe that transparency begins with honest comparisons and no hidden data. Here, the audience can easily compare the years to one another and see how the GHG emissions per person has changed.
  • We even added a legend so that the reader cannot get confused as to what trend the line is depicting.
  • We also added interaction to the graph! When a user highlights one of the graph points, information regarding said point is presented; In this case, it is the year, the aggregate amount of emission per person (by the thousand kg) that is presented, and the change in emission from the previous year
  • All the titling is clear and straight to the point as to what the visualization is for.


Black Hat Visualization Explanation:

Our Black Hat Visualization included the following manipulative techniques:
> Cherry-picking the data where the greatest emission drop was seen (2018-2020), and basing the visualization solely on this subset to exaggerate the decline.
> Our bubbles were exaggerated in size by going by using a cubic bubble-size scale to make the decline more drastic
> The added transition movement of the bubbles take the user's attention and draw attention to the large decrease, persuading the viewer that a significant decline in emissions has occurred.
> We also chose a spiral display over a time-series chart for a harder comparison of the data
We used misleading/vague labels (such as "Earlier", "Recent", "Latest") to hide the true 3-year subset data span
> Our visualization is also titled with a misleading headine that makes users think pollution levels have stayed at a decline, however after 2020 in our dataset, the emissions rise again

However, the user can use the tooltip to over over each circle of the visualization to see truly the emission range is not that drastic (10.2-11.9) and the years being tracked (2018-2020).

Development Process:

For development we split the tasks by the two visualizations, meaning one person was in charge of the White Hate Visualization and the other was in charge of the Black Hat Visualization. Given we have an init() function and two svg canvases defined, development was simple as we each created a distinct function for our visualization and called it in the init() function (also appended to our corresponding svg canvas). Each visualization took roughly 4-5 hours, with an additional 1-2 hours for write-ups, deployemnts, and submission work. Planning and figuring out how to use the data took the most time, especially for the Black Hat Visualization, in terms of finding a suitable subset of the data to highlight a desired outcome.

For the Black Hat Visualization, we derived implementation details from our previous spiral Lab 7 and the vibe coding from Lab 8. We also utilized the AI Coding Tool Codex to assist with the implementation, stylistic additions, and debugging of the visualization. Examples of AI usage include the the pop-up tool box for each data point and the beginning animation when the graph first loads in.
